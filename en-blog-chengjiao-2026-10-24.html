Business Pain Points · Closing Deals

Clients Keep Saying
“Let Me Think About It”?

They never say no. They just never say yes — and then they vanish

Three months of talks. Eight revisions of the proposal. Price cut to the bone. And at the finish line the client says, “Let me think about it” — and goes quiet. You follow up, politely, and nothing moves.

You think it is the price? You already cut it. You think it is the product? A more expensive competitor closed the same client.

The deal did not die on price. It died on the last breath. The client never settled while sitting in your office — and once they walked out, everything else scattered their decision.

That is the most unjust way to lose a deal: the client likes you, the budget exists, the decision-maker has met you — and it still slips away on the final inch. More sales talk and more discounting at this point is effort spent in the wrong place.

“Wrong feng shui, wasted effort.” When deals stall at the close, nine times out of ten the negotiation room and the reception space are leaking qi — people cannot sit still, and minds cannot settle.

🎯 Three Ways a Close Dies

Way one: the negotiator's back faces the door — the lead negotiator sits with their back to the entrance or the corridor, qi draining from behind. Restless seat, lost negotiation before it starts.

Way two: the meeting room is a wind tunnel — door facing a window, door facing the elevator. Qi rushes in and straight out again; the client cannot settle, and ten minutes in they already want to leave.

Way three: the boss's seat carries no weight — a weak boss's office projects no authority, and the client senses, without knowing why, that “this company feels shaky.” So they choose to think about it — forever.

📉 A Real Case: A Big Deal Stuck at Signing for Six Months

An equipment supplier talked a major deal for six months. Samples tested, price agreed, contract revised five times — stuck at the signature.

I walked the floor: the meeting room door faced the elevator, and the negotiation table had its back to the entrance. The lead negotiator had been pitching from a leaking seat every single day.

Not a pitch problem — a position problem. The seat moved to a solid wall facing the lively sector, the meeting room door was re-angled, and the deal signed the following month.

✦My Approach

Step One: The Negotiator's Seat — Back to a Solid Wall

The lead negotiator must sit with a solid wall behind and face the lively sector. A supported back steadies the mind; a steady seat steadies the words. Negotiations are won on terms, yes — but half the leverage comes from the seat itself.

Step Two: Make the Meeting Room Hold Qi — So Clients Do Not Want to Leave

Block the through-draft, re-angle the door, neutralize the afflictions. When clients sit comfortably and feel settled, “let me think about it” becomes hard to say. Many deals are not talked into being — they are sat into being.

Step Three: A Boss's Seat With Weight — Trust at First Sight

The boss's office is the company's anchor. Strong qi there, and clients feel at the door that this company can be trusted with their money. Weak qi, and even the best proposal gets discounted.

✦Do the Math

A lost deal costs more than one order's profit — six months of follow-up, the team's time, and the market window.

Costlier still is the contagion of “let me think about it”: one stalling client drains the sales team's morale, three stalling clients wreck the whole quarter's rhythm.

Instead of polishing the pitch year after year, get the negotiation room right first.
